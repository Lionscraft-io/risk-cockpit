# Language and failure modes

*Phrases that work, phrases to handle carefully, the seven ways this story breaks, and what is deliberately unresolved.*

> Part of the [Programmable Risk Transfer narrative](README.md). Sections 41–45 of the master brief, unaltered.

---

# 41. The story should never collapse these levels

Wrong:

> We are building a Sri Lanka telecom disaster product using blockchain and AI.

Wrong:

> We are building tokenised ILS.

Wrong:

> We are building an AI insurance administration platform.

Wrong:

> We are building emergency-response software.

Better:

> **We are building programmable risk-transfer infrastructure.**
>
> **Disaster finance is the first application.**
>
> **Response-Linked ART is the first product.**
>
> **Sri Lanka telecom is the first implementation.**

# 42. Language that currently works

Strong phrases:

> **Risk is the possibility of future suffering.**

> **Risk is transferred. Capital is the vehicle. Protection is the purpose.**

> **The instrument itself can carry more of the purpose of protection.**

> **The output is not an app. The output is a working risk-transfer facility.**

> **Risk should tend to flow toward capacity.**

> **Capital can become a richer computational object.**

> **As life becomes more machine-legible, risk becomes more machine-legible too.**

> **The minimum economically transferable unit of risk can fall.**

> **Digital assets give intangible risk relationships financial form.**

> **AI changes the economics of complexity.**

> **Not another insurance app. A new operating system for risk-bearing capital.**

# 43. Language to use carefully

## “Transform suffering”

Conceptually powerful, but potentially too grand if used without explanation.

Better in technical contexts:

- reduce harm;
- change consequences;
- reduce realised loss;
- shorten disruption;
- improve recovery;
- alter the pathway from risk to outcome.

## “Decentralise suffering”

Interesting philosophical language.

But suffering itself remains experienced by people.

More precise:

- decentralise burden-bearing;
- redistribute exposure;
- distribute the financial consequences of risk;
- distribute responsibility and capacity.

## “AI-native”

Useful for conceptual discussions.

Avoid implying AI is conscious or literally a lifeform.

Prefer:

> AI is computationally native to machine-legible environments.

## “Programmable”

Do not allow it to become empty marketing language.

Always be able to answer:

> What exactly becomes programmable?

Possible answers:

- rights;
- obligations;
- capital states;
- settlement;
- permitted uses;
- activation conditions;
- claims;
- evidence requirements.

# 44. Common storytelling failure modes

## Failure 1 — Starting with the pilot

If the deck begins with Sri Lanka, telecom outages or disaster-response funding, the reader assumes that is the company.

## Failure 2 — Making technology the protagonist

“AI + blockchain + insurance” is not the story.

Risk is the protagonist.

Technology changes what risk transfer can become.

## Failure 3 — Being too philosophical without showing the thing

The vision can become compelling but intangible.

Slides 7–8 must show the actual system.

## Failure 4 — Being too concrete too early

Starting immediately with triggers, tranches, suppliers and settlement loses the larger reason the architecture matters.

## Failure 5 — Attacking traditional insurance

The project extends the expressive capacity of risk transfer.

It does not need to pretend centuries of insurance innovation are useless.

## Failure 6 — Presenting AI only as administration

That makes AI appear bolted on.

Administration is the **current application** of a technology whose future role is managing increasingly machine-legible complexity.

## Failure 7 — Presenting digital assets only as settlement technology

Digital assets matter because financial objects themselves can become programmable, stateful and increasingly granular.

## Failure 8 — Claiming the future already exists

The deck must distinguish:

**what is buildable now**

from

**what the architecture may ultimately enable.**

# 45. Open questions that do not need to be solved in the main story yet

The narrative can remain stable while several implementation questions continue evolving.

These include:

- exact legal form of facilities;
- when an SPV is required;
- whether particular implementations use notes, insurance, reinsurance, deposits or tokenised claims;
- exact jurisdiction;
- exact custody model;
- investor eligibility;
- precise tranche structures;
- trigger design;
- regulatory treatment;
- whether facilities are fully on-chain, partially on-chain or use tokenised representations;
- governance of automated state changes;
- exact boundary between automated administration and human decisions;
- outcome-verification standards;
- pricing methodology;
- basis-risk management;
- which functions Lionscraft performs directly versus through regulated partners.

Do not allow unresolved implementation details to destabilise the core narrative.
